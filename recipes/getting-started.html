Getting Started with Sourdough

One method, three steps, a lot of waiting. Read this once — every bake on this site is a variation on it.

Sourdough sounds like a project: feeding schedules, exact timings, a starter you have to keep alive like a pet. It isn’t. My whole method is three steps with a lot of waiting in between — and every recipe on this site is just a variation on this one story. This is how I make my sourdough loaf.

Ingredients

Sourdough starter
100g
(Whole wheat) flour
450g
Water
300g
Salt
8g

I bake my loaves with 100% whole wheat flour, or 100% whole spelt. You can also go 85% whole grain, or mix in some white flour. The flour you pick decides how much water the dough wants — but you’ll learn to feel that as you go (too dry, too wet) and adjust. Since it’s practically the only ingredient in the loaf, I always go for the best organic flour I can find, from a local producer.

I use plain tap water, straight from the tap.

And whatever salt is on hand. Coarse or fine — it dissolves in the dough either way.

The starter

Try to get a starter from a friend who’s properly into sourdough. Or make your own — there are plenty of guides online.

For this recipe we start from 100g of active starter: 50g whole wheat flour and 50g water. What “active” means exactly, you’ll find out by doing, in the steps below.

The method

This is how I bake my sourdough loaves. I’ve kept the process as simple as I could. Consider it a first handhold, not a rulebook — feel free to let go of it whenever you like.

1 · Set the loaf going

Take a big pot with a lid, and take your active starter out of the fridge (how it ended up there — see step 2). Spoon all of the starter into the pot.

Now feed the starter: the jar it lived in still has some goo on the inside, and that goo carries more than enough microbes to start a fresh colony. Add 50g whole wheat flour and 50g water, and stir it through with the same spoon. The starter is fed now, but not active yet. Put the lid back on the jar and leave it on the counter.

Into the pot go 450g flour, 300g water and 8g salt — stir with the same spoon. After a minute of stirring you should have a proper ball of dough: no loose flour left, and not too runny either. Too dry? Add a little water. Too wet? Add a little flour. Lid on the pot, and set it on the counter next to the starter.

Now the last ingredient makes its entrance: time. Wait a few hours — anywhere from four to twelve, depending on the room temperature.

2 · Fold

The dough in the pot has doubled in volume, and so has the starter. That’s when the starter is active. You’ll recognise it by the gas bubbles and that vinegary, sour smell.

With an active starter you can either set a new loaf going (step 1), or put the starter in the fridge. In the fridge the microbes stop multiplying — the starter goes on pause. It stays alive and ready for your next bake, and it’ll survive weeks in there without any fuss.

Dust your work surface with flour and turn the risen dough out onto it. Fold it like this: take the right side of the dough and fold it to the left, landing about two-thirds across. Then do the same from the left. Then take the top and fold it down, and finally the bottom and fold it up. Like folding a T-shirt. Repeat a few rounds — each one gets harder as the stretch runs out. The moment you feel the dough would tear: stop. You’re done. I fold three or four rounds at most, which takes under two minutes. Flip the ball over so all the folds end up underneath, and round it into a neat ball.

Take a big bowl the dough fits in, in whatever shape you like — round, long, whatever. It decides the final shape of your loaf. A banneton works too. Line it with a kitchen towel, set the dough ball on top (folds still underneath), fold the towel over so the dough doesn’t dry out, and put it in the fridge. The cold proof can run anywhere from a few hours to a full two days — and it’s where the loaf picks up its rich flavour.

3 · Bake

Preheat the oven to 250°C and try to build up a good amount of steam. If your oven has a steam bake function, use it. Otherwise, put a bowl of water in the oven, or bake the loaf in a cloche.

Once the oven is hot, take the dough out of the fridge and tip it onto a baking sheet lined with parchment — folds-side up. The bread will burst open right along those folds and form a delicious crust.

Into the oven, and immediately down to 200°C. Bake for 40 minutes. You’ll see the loaf roughly double in the oven — that’s exactly why the steam matters: the crust mustn’t set too early.

Take the loaf out and let it cool on a rack.

Fitting it into a normal week

Three steps, and a lot of time in between. It can look overwhelming. It doesn’t have to be. Here’s how I fit it into a regular work week. Say we start on a Monday evening.

Monday evening, after dinner: I set my loaf going. The starter is active and in the fridge, and flour, water and salt are on hand — this takes literally five minutes. And if you were paying attention: after this step there’s exactly one spoon to wash.

Tuesday morning, twelve hours later: the dough has risen and is ready to fold. Another five minutes or so, though now there’s also a work surface to wipe down. The folded loaf goes in the fridge, together with the active starter.

Tuesday evening, after dinner: I bake. This step takes 40 minutes — but unless it’s your very first sourdough loaf, you’re not going to spend those 40 minutes staring at the oven.

Wednesday morning: you’ve got the loaf you started on Monday evening.

In total, making a loaf costs a good ten minutes of actual work.
